# The Silence Between Stars

{% stardate "7", "203", "02:14" %}

{% shipAlert "info", "Transmission queued for relay. Estimated Earth receipt: 14.3 years." %}

It has been 214 days since I last heard a human voice that wasn't a recording.

I keep a playlist — messages from my sister, a few old voicemails I never deleted, a birthday video from the crew of the *Helios Station* before departure. I ration them like water in a desert crossing: one per week, never more, or I'll hollow them out entirely and have nothing left to hold onto.

Tonight was different, though. The ship's passive arrays flagged an anomaly at 1,420 MHz — hydrogen line. The frequency every physicist agrees any spacefaring species would recognize. My heart did something embarrassing when the alert chimed. I actually laughed out loud — an ugly, startled sound that echoed in the command module and felt strange in my own ears.

I ran the analysis three times. Then four. The signal was narrow-band, 47 seconds in duration, repeating with a 72-second gap. 

My hands wouldn't stop shaking.

By the third hour I had triangulated the source: a magnetar 340 light-years off the port bow, cycling through its characteristic spin-down. Astrophysics, not archaeology. Not contact. The universe doing what the universe always does — producing patterns that look like meaning from a sufficient distance, then dissolving into physics when you get close enough to look properly.

I made tea. Oolong, the last of the good stuff. I watched the magnetar's signature roll across my screen in slow green waves and thought about how the line between signal and noise is mostly a matter of expectation.

Outside, the stars don't twinkle here. No atmosphere to scatter the light. They just hold perfectly still, cold and bright, like eyes that have seen everything and found it unremarkable. I've started to understand how ancient sailors felt watching a shoreline recede — that specific vertigo of choosing to move away from everything familiar, then living inside that choice every waking hour.

I miss gravity that isn't manufactured. I miss weather. I miss the smell of rain on pavement so badly some mornings it physically aches.

But then *Perihelion* adjusts her heading by three degrees and a new arm of the galaxy slides into view through the forward cameras, and I forget, briefly, how far I am from home. There's so much out here. So much that no human eye has ever traced. 

That has to be enough. Most days, it is.

*— Mr. Collin Mizner, Explorer Vessel Perihelion*
