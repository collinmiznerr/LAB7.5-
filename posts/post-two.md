# A Planet No One Named

{% stardate "7", "251", "18:47" %}

{% shipAlert "warning", "Orbital insertion burn in T-minus 6 hours. Radiation shielding at 94%." %}

The catalog designation is GJ 1061 d. I've started calling her Cinder.

She hangs in the viewport like a burnt ember — tidally locked, one hemisphere facing the red dwarf in permanent daylight, the other side buried in a darkness so total my instruments insist it's colder than space itself. From this altitude she looks wrong somehow, like a coin someone left half in a fire. The day side glows a dim, rust-orange. The night side is a rumor.

But in the terminator band — that narrow ribbon of perpetual twilight where the two hemispheres meet — I spotted something that stopped my breath.

Movement. Atmospheric. Enormous convection cells churning where hot air bleeds into cold, throwing up cloud formations that spiral and collapse in slow, clockwork loops. From 800 kilometers up they look like fingerprints. Like a signature left by no one in particular, or perhaps by physics itself, pleased with its own handiwork.

I've been in low orbit for thirty-one hours now. Perihelion's sensors have mapped the terminator zone in enough detail to fill a dozen doctoral theses. Surface temperature: a relatively stable 4°C to 11°C in the habitable band. Atmospheric pressure: 1.3 bar. Nitrogen-dominant, with traces of CO₂ and something the mass spectrometer keeps flagging as complex organics. I've run that analysis six times. Still complex organics.

I'm not saying what I'm thinking. Not yet. Not in an official log.

What I will say is this: I stood at the forward viewport for an hour this evening and watched those cloud spirals turn, and I felt something I haven't felt since I left the solar system. Not loneliness. Not longing. Something more like reverence.

Naming things is a human itch — we can't look at something without wanting to put a word on it, claim it somehow with language. GJ 1061 d doesn't need my name for it. It was doing exactly this, these slow spiraling clouds, these terminator storms, this patient tidal breathing, for four billion years before any creature on Earth learned to look up and wonder. It will be doing this long after whatever I write in this log has crumbled to noise.

I'll send the spectrometry data in the next relay burst. Let someone else figure out what the organics mean.

For now I'm going to make bad instant coffee and watch a planet exist. It seems like the right thing to do.

*— Dr. Mira Vasquez, Explorer Vessel Perihelion*
